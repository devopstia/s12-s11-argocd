One caveat to say out loud

The two series are not measured identically. The S&P figures are gross of dividend withholding tax and the MSCI figures are net of it, which understates the global series by roughly 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points a year. Published gross and net series exist for both indexes, but not consistently back to 2001, so this page uses what each provider publishes as its headline series and tells you which is which. When the gap between the two indexes is small, that difference in measurement is large enough to matter.

How to check any of these yourself

Four steps, no subscription required.

  1. Pick a year from the table above. Say 2022, where the table shows the S&P 500 at −18.1% and the global market at −18.4%.
  2. Open the Slickcharts annual returns page linked above. It lists S&P 500 total return for every calendar year back to 1926. Find the year and compare. The figures on this page are rounded to one decimal, so 2022 reads −18.11% there and −18.1% here.
  3. Open the MSCI index page linked above and choose the ACWI index. Its performance section publishes annual returns for the net series in U.S. dollars.
  4. For a second opinion on the S&P series, the NYU Stern dataset gives annual returns from 1928 onward, compiled independently. It runs a year behind, so it will not yet cover the most recent year.

How this site turns those figures into the numbers you see

Three conventions, stated plainly, because they are the difference between two answers that both look right.

Average year

Add the yearly returns and divide by the number of years. It describes a typical year and is the number most often quoted, but no investor ever earns it. It is always the highest of the three.

Annualized return

The constant yearly rate that turns the starting value into the ending value: the compound annual growth rate. This is what a lump sum left alone actually earned, and it is always below the average year whenever returns move around.

What a plan earned

The constant rate that turns a specific schedule of contributions into the ending balance. It can land either side of the index rate depending on whether the money arrived before or after the strong years.

The arithmetic

  • Contributions land at the end of each month and compound monthly, on both the projection tab and the history tab, so the two are directly comparable.
  • Inside a calendar year, the index return is spread evenly across the twelve months using the twelfth root of the annual figure. Only annual index returns are published here, so month to month timing is smoothed out. The real months inside 2008 were nothing like each other, and a plan that happened to buy in November of that year would have done better than this page shows. Over a full year the ending figure is very close; over a single year it is an approximation, and it is the only one on the site.
  • Nothing is deducted. No fund expense ratio, no trading costs, no taxes, no advice fee. A real investor holding VOO or VT pays a small expense ratio, so real results run slightly below these figures.
  • You cannot buy an index. The ETFs named here are examples of funds that track these indexes; they are not recommendations, and their returns differ from the index by the cost of tracking it.

A side-by-side view of two ways to own the stock market: the S&P 500 and the global stock market. The projection tab models a monthly contribution at a rate you choose.
